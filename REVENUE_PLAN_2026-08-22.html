02

The kirana problem

Your product is named after your weakest market

This is the finding I'd most expect you to push back on, so here is the whole argument in one place.

Kirana has the largest market and the worst economics of the eleven. Thirteen million shops sounds like the prize, but every input that turns a shop into revenue points the wrong way. Willingness to pay is the lowest of any trade, so blended price lands at ₹4,149. Engagement is the lowest, so churn is the highest at 30%. And it is the hardest trade to reach — no association, no cluster, no distributor relationship, thirteen million individual doors — so CAC carries the highest difficulty multiplier in the model, ₹7,000 a shop.

That yields an LTV of ₹11,756 against a CAC of ₹7,000. 1.7×. Payback takes 24 months on a customer who is 30% likely to leave inside twelve. A field rep selling kirana destroys value on every close.

And the competitor isn't myBillBook at ₹3,490. It's Vyapar's mobile tier, which is permanently free and already does GST invoicing, ledgers, payment reminders and offline billing. Your offline-correctness engineering is far better than theirs. A kirana owner cannot see that difference and will not pay ₹4,999 a year for it.

What to do instead

Keep kirana, but change what it is. Make it a free or ₹999 self-serve tier with no install fee and no field sales — sign-up, import a catalogue, start billing, all without a human. Let it earn its keep three other ways: as the top of the funnel for shops that grow into a paid trade, as the volume that makes your catalogue and credit data valuable, and as the reference base that makes the other ten trades believable.

What it must not be is a line item in anybody's sales target. The model has kirana at 3–5% of new shops throughout, all inbound. That is deliberate.

One consequence worth naming: if kirana becomes a free tier, "KiranaOS" is the wrong name for the paying product. A restaurant owner being sold ₹14,999 software called KiranaOS is being told, in the first three seconds, that they are not the customer. That's a cheap thing to fix now and an expensive one to fix at 2,000 shops.

03

What to charge

The single highest-leverage change in this plan

Your current price list is internally consistent and externally mispriced. It was built as a ladder off the kirana anchor — every trade priced as a premium over ₹2,499 — when it should have been built off each trade's own incumbent. Here is what the market comparison implies.

Test it before you believe it

The proposed prices are not in the workbook — the model ships with your current price list so the baseline is yours, not mine. To see the effect, edit columns C, D and E on the Unit Economics sheet. Everything downstream, including both three-year cases, recalculates.

On the ₹4,999 launch service

Keep it for restaurant, pharmacy, auto parts and electronics — those shops genuinely need catalogue entry, printer setup and training, and they'll pay for it. Drop it entirely for kirana self-serve, where it's the single largest barrier to a free signup.

But watch its share of revenue. In the base case it is 57% of year-one revenue and still 38% by year three. That is services income carrying a person's day, at roughly 40% margin, and an acquirer will value that part of your business on an EBITDA multiple rather than a revenue multiple. The Summary sheet tracks the ratio; keep it heading down.

04

The sequence

Three phases, and what each one has to prove before the next starts

Now → Q1 2027

Prove it in restaurants

One trade, one city, no new features. The goal is not revenue — it is five reference shops that have run a full month of service without you in the room.

  • Close the release gate: CI run, printer certification, automated backups
  • 5 pilot restaurants, discounted, in one cluster you can drive to
  • Instrument everything: bill time, print failures, sync conflicts, support calls
  • Then raise restaurant pricing to ₹14,999 and sell the next 20 at full price
2027

Add pharmacy and auto parts

Two trades with the best retention and the least competition, sold through channels rather than door-to-door.

  • Pharmacy via AIOCD district associations and pharma distributors
  • Auto parts via the physical clusters — one market at a time
  • Ship e-invoicing and e-way bills; without them you cannot sell to larger shops
  • Launch kirana self-serve as a free funnel with zero sales cost
  • Target: ~300 active shops, ₹21 L exit ARR
2028 →

Widen, or raise

The fork in the plan. At roughly 600 shops your engineering and support base pays for itself, and you choose whether to grow on your own cash or somebody else's.

  • Base: 5 reps, add electronics and clothing, ~650 shops by end of 2029
  • Funded: raise ₹2.5–3 Cr, 40+ reps, ~4,500 shops, ₹3 Cr ARR
  • Either way: monetise the udhar ledger through lending referral
  • Either way: cut to the trades you can actually certify

How you actually reach each trade

This is the part most POS plans skip, and it's the part that decides whether the CAC numbers hold. The trades differ enormously in how reachable they are, and that difference is already priced into the model's CAC multipliers.

Two things worth reading off this rather than past it.

The base case is not comfortable. ₹39 lakh of cumulative burn over three years is real money for a solo founder, even with your own draw modelled at ₹40,000 a month rather than market rate. If that runway doesn't exist, the lever is the hiring plan on the Base Case sheet — fewer reps means slower growth and a smaller hole, and the model will show you the exact trade.

Neither case is EBITDA-positive by year three, and that's correct rather than a modelling error. Both cross the point where engineering and support are covered by subscription revenue; both are then deliberately spending the surplus on sales because LTV/CAC in the lead trades is 6–9×. Spending into that ratio is the right decision, and it's what makes the year-four picture very different from the year-three one.

Where this model is most likely wrong

Closes per rep. Everything scales off 8 closes per rep per month. That's achievable in a dense restaurant cluster and optimistic almost everywhere else. If the real number is 5, every revenue figure falls by around 40%. Measure it with your own first rep before hiring a second.

Churn. All eleven rates are estimates — you have no renewal data, because you have no customers. Pharmacy at 12% and kirana at 30% are directional judgements about how embedded the software becomes, not observations. Your first renewal cohort will move these more than any other input.

Outlet counts. Kirana and pharmacy are well sourced; restaurant is a defensible industry estimate; the other eight are order-of-magnitude guesses flagged Low on the Market Sizing sheet. This matters less than it looks, because the ranking is driven by price headroom and retention rather than by market size — and because sales capacity, not market size, is what actually limits revenue in every scenario here.

About the model

The workbook is fully live — 1,106 formulas, no hardcoded outputs, verified to recalculate without errors. Blue cells are inputs, yellow cells are the assumptions that move the answer most. Pricing comes from your own docs/PRICING_BUNDLE.md; competitor pricing was checked on 22 August 2026. Petpooja does not publish rates, so those are reported ranges rather than vendor-confirmed figures.

Revenue is built bottom-up from headcount and close rates, not top-down from market size. Every market figure here exists to rank trades against each other. This is a planning model for arguing with, not a forecast, and not financial advice.
