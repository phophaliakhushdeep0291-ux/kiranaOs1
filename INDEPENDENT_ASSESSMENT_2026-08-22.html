01

What it's worth

Three lenses, three very different numbers

There is no single number, because "worth" means three unrelated things and this product sits in a different place on each one. Worth stating plainly up front: none of the value is currently blocked by missing code. It is blocked by having zero shops on the system.

LensValueWhat it actually measures
Replacement cost ₹1.0–1.6 Cr Five engineers (2 backend, 2 frontend, 1 QA) for ~14 months at Indian loaded rates. At US contract rates the same build is $700k–1.2M. This number is falling — you built it in 74 days with AI assistance, and so can the next person.
Asset sale, today ₹8–25 L What a buyer with existing distribution — a POS reseller, a payments company, a CA-software firm — would pay for the codebase plus your involvement. No revenue, no users, one author. Below that band, the maintenance liability makes it worth roughly nothing to a buyer without a team.
As a business, 500 shops ₹0.9–1.5 Cr At your own pricing, 500 mixed-vertical shops ≈ ₹30 L ARR. Indian SMB SaaS below $1M ARR trades at roughly 1–3× revenue, not the 2.5–4× the US bands quote.
As a business, 2,000 shops ₹3.5–6 Cr ≈ ₹1.2 Cr ARR with retention proven over two renewal cycles. This is the first point where the product's engineering advantage starts being something a buyer pays for.

Not financial advice — I'm not a valuer, and any real number depends on retention, churn and a diligence process none of us have run. These are ranges to argue with, not a price.

The multiple trap

Your ₹4,999 first-year launch service is services revenue, not SaaS. It carries a person's time and gets an EBITDA multiple (2–4×), not a revenue multiple. If it ends up as a large share of turnover, the whole business gets valued as a services firm. Keep it visible and separate in the books, and keep it under ~30% of total revenue at scale.

What moves each number

The asset number moves on proof: one green CI certification, printer certification on the two supported models, and 5 pilot shops running for 60 days would roughly double it, because it converts "impressive repository" into "de-risked product." The business number moves only on distribution: shops signed, renewed, and referring. Nothing in the codebase moves either one right now.

02

Is it reliable

Strong build, unproven in the field — two different questions

Reliability in a POS means two things and they need separating. Correctness — does the money add up, does an offline bill survive a reload, does a retry duplicate a ledger entry. On this, the code is genuinely strong, and I verified the mechanisms rather than taking the docs' word. Field reliability — does it survive a real shop, a real printer, a real power cut, for real months. Here there is no evidence at all, because it has never been in a shop.

Verified strong

  • Money arithmetic runs in integer paise. utils/money.js converts to paise, sums, and rounds once — no accumulating float drift in calculations.
  • Conflicts are classified by entity, not last-write-wins. Products, customers and suppliers allow an owner-audited resolution; bills, payments, udhar and stock ledgers are forced through reversal entries. Money can't be overwritten.
  • Idempotency is bound to payload content. A purchase-receipt retry key is tied to the PO, payment, invoice and line quantities; a print job is bound to the SHA-256 of its receipt. A reused key with changed content is rejected, not replayed.
  • Audits commit inside the mutation's transaction, with fault-injection tests proving the mutation rolls back when the audit insert fails.
  • Restore drill reconciles exactly. 1,781 records across 81 tables restored in 0.6s at 0 paise and 0 stock-unit variance; tamper and truncation are refused.
  • Owner PINs are recursively redacted from conflict snapshots before they're persisted or displayed.

Genuinely open

  • No CI certification exists for any commit. Run #316 was refused by GitHub over a failed account payment. Every green result you have is local, from a dirty working tree.
  • No printer has been physically certified. Two models are targeted; neither has a paper-out, disconnect, power-cut or retry artifact. This is the single most common way a POS fails in a shop.
  • No automated daily backup, so the ≤24h recovery objective isn't met in production.
  • BUG-040 is "Fixed", not "Verified" — the cashier-versus-owner approval flow hasn't been run live. It's a P0 about discount authorisation, which is exactly where staff fraud happens.
  • Bus factor is one. 274k lines, one author, no external contributors. This is the biggest single discount any buyer will apply.

Two findings the documentation doesn't flag

  • Medium 85 money columns are still stored as floats The schema has 197 Float fields, of which ~85 hold money — Bill.grandTotal, paidAmount, lineTotal, tax fields, supplier dues. The "Phase 27 paise migration" added shadow BigInt columns to only four of them plus the snapshot and subscription tables. Your own code comment is honest about it: "DB columns remain Float for now." In practice the risk is low — a double holds 2-decimal rupee values exactly well past any shop's lifetime turnover, and all arithmetic already routes through paise. But it blocks any audit-grade claim, and any raw SUM() in SQL bypasses your paise helpers entirely. Finish the migration before you sell to anyone whose accountant asks.
  • Medium The bundle is too heavy for the phones you're targeting Your own production check reports 4,719.6 kB of JavaScript raw / 1,314.1 kB gzipped, with the restaurant vertical's offline payload at 3,416.6 kB raw / 1,011.6 kB gzipped across 181 files. That is a slow, memory-hungry first install on a ₹7,000 Android over a 3G connection — which is precisely the hardware a kirana counter runs. The tests all pass on your laptop. Aim for ≤150 kB gzipped at startup and ≤400 kB per vertical payload.
The honest summary

If I were buying this, I'd believe the correctness work — the paise reconciliation, the entity-classified conflict handling and the transaction-bound audits are better than what I'd expect from a Series-A team, let alone one person. I would not yet believe it's reliable, because reliability is a claim about shops, and there are none. Those are compatible statements, and the gap between them is about six weeks of unglamorous work, not a rewrite.

Three things this table says

In kirana, you are priced above the market anchor. Starter at ₹2,499 undercuts Vyapar and myBillBook — but Growth at ₹4,999 plus the ₹4,999 install puts year one near ₹10,000, roughly 2.5–3× what a shopkeeper has been told billing software costs. And the honest competitor isn't myBillBook's ₹3,490; it's Vyapar's permanently free mobile tier, which already does GST invoicing, ledgers and offline billing. Your offline-correctness engineering is far better than theirs. Shopkeepers cannot see that, and won't pay for it.

In restaurants, the picture completely inverts. Petpooja runs ₹36,000–₹1.44 lakh a year before add-ons, doesn't publish its pricing, and charges again for Zomato/Swiggy integration. Restaurant Growth at ₹8,999/yr is a 4–16× undercut against an incumbent whose customers routinely complain about hidden fees. This is the strongest commercial position in the entire product — and you've already instinctively acted on it by forking restaurant-pos out on its own. That instinct is right. I'd go further and make it the primary product.

Square and Toast aren't comparators, they're a warning. They can give software away at $0 because they earn 2.4–3.09% on every payment. That model is structurally unavailable to you: UPI merchant transactions in India carry effectively zero MDR. Software plus services is your only revenue line, which permanently caps ARPU and makes the ₹4,999 install fee necessary rather than optional. It also means the interesting money has to come from somewhere else — see the udhar ledger idea below.

Where you genuinely beat everyone in your price band

Nobody selling at ₹3,500/year does entity-classified conflict resolution, paise-exact restore drills, transaction-bound audit trails, or an eleven-vertical pack system. Nobody at ₹36,000/year does either. That is a real and defensible engineering lead.

The problem is that it's invisible at the point of sale. A shopkeeper evaluates: how fast can I bill, will my CA accept the GST output, and will someone answer the phone at 9pm. Your answers to the first two are strong; the third is the ₹4,999 launch service, which is the right idea and also the thing that doesn't scale without people on the ground.

Where you're behind

Against myBillBook Enterprise: no e-invoicing (IRN) and no e-way bills. That's a hard ceiling on the size of shop you can sell to, and the mandatory turnover threshold keeps dropping. Against Petpooja: Zomato/Swiggy channel integrations exist only partially (there's channel-settlement and Flipkart plumbing), and there's no KDS hardware story. Against everyone: field support, brand, and a reference customer.
